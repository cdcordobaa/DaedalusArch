\begin{abstract}
LLM judges are increasingly placed inside software quality gates, next to deterministic rules. We report ten
lessons from one such gate: \tool{}, an architectural conformance checker for TypeScript whose score combines 25
symbolic fitness functions with two rubric-driven LLM judge functions. The evaluation was pre-registered and every
judge call was recorded. On nine real projects the judge failed about a fifth of the units it saw, yet under the
registered weight of 8\% it changed no verdict; on 65 agent-generated projects it changed none either. We show why
with a simple algebraic bound and a judge-blind decomposition, and we show that the aggregation rule and the weight,
not the judge, decide whether it matters: with a weight of 2/7 and an any-fail rule it moves 8 of 9 real-project
verdicts. A clean judge dimension even raises the composite score. Coding the judge's rationales over-counts
criteria because the judge names criteria while negating them. Most judge failures coincide with rule findings, a
self-preference signal turns out to be confounded with code quality, and a cross-family LLM panel agrees with the
judge on 34 of 36 items (weighted agreement 0.982) with an AC1 of 0.98 but a $\kappa$ of 0, because the judge never failed a sampled generated
unit. Replaying 100 recorded units with a second model of the same vendor changes no verdict (agreement 0.94,
$\kappa = 0.77$); the frozen judge is slightly stricter. Our synthetic judge probes place no seed in real or
generated code, so the judge's detection rate could not be measured. We close with operational lessons on CLI
isolation, an unblockable web-search tool and fail-closed guards, and with recommendations for anyone who puts an
LLM judge inside a gate. A follow-up catch test on planted anti-patterns (development data) finds that the judge
fails 30 of 35 seeded units while failing 4 of 50 twins, but its catch on duplicated rules is confounded with a
misplaced-method cue; deterministic proxies catch type-level leaks the judge misses, and the judge catches
duplications the proxies' filters remove. We argue for a layered gate in which rules decide, cheap proxies propose and
the judge interprets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Architecture conformance checking compares the code with an intended architecture
\cite{murphy1995reflexion}, and architectural fitness functions turn that comparison into automated checks
\citeneeded{ford2017evolutionary}. Rules express structure well (which layer may import which) but say little about
intent: whether a file's responsibilities fit its layer, or whether a module keeps one invariant in one place. An
LLM judge is the obvious complement, and LLM-as-a-judge is now a common evaluation pattern
\cite{zheng2023judging}\citeneeded{liu2023geval,gu2024survey}, also for code \citeneeded{zhuo2024icescore}.

Most reports on LLM judges ask whether the judge agrees with humans. Inside a gate a second question comes first:
\emph{can the judge change the outcome at all?} A gate turns many signals into one verdict (here pass, warning,
soft-block or hard-block), and the way the judge's output is aggregated and weighted can make its accuracy
irrelevant. This paper reports what we learned from one such gate, \tool{}, during a pre-registered evaluation
\cite{nosek2018prereg}. Every judge call was recorded in a cassette, so every reading below was recomputed from
the same calls with no new judge call.

Our contribution is a set of twelve lessons (ten from the registered evaluation, two from a v1.3 follow-up on development data), each with its evidence: (1) the judge was inert, and a simple bound
shows why; (2) aggregation and weight decide whether it matters; (3) a clean judge dimension inflates the
composite score; (4) keyword coding of judge rationales misleads; (5) most judge failures overlap with rule
findings; (6) a self-preference signal is confounded with code quality; (7) validating the judge with an LLM panel
gives high agreement but leaves fail detection untested; (8) the verdicts are robust to the judge model, with
the frozen judge slightly stricter; (9) synthetic judge probes may have no construct validity, because they find
no site in real or generated code; (10) isolating CLI-based judges and labellers is harder than it looks;
(11) a v1.3 catch test with redesigned operators shows the judge catches most planted anti-patterns, but a twin that
adds a member confounds the catch; (12) deterministic proxies, the judge and the symbolic rules see different
things, which motivates a layered gate (Section~\ref{sec:v13}). Section~\ref{sec:recs} turns them into recommendations.

\section{The Gate and the Judge}\label{sec:gate}

\paragraph{Score and verdict.}
\tool{} compiles an architecture specification into fitness functions over a graph of the code. Each function
belongs to one of seven dimensions. For a dimension $d$ the Architectural Violation Ratio $\mathrm{AVR}_d \in [0,1]$
is the confidence-weighted share of its executed functions that fail, and the Architectural Health Score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ahs}
  \mathrm{AHS} = 1 - \sum_d w_d\, \mathrm{AVR}_d, \qquad \textstyle\sum_d w_d = 1 .
\end{equation}
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.5 ("the AHS formula (round3(1 - sum w * AVR))")
$\ahs{det}$ uses the five symbolic dimensions only; $\ahs{comb}$ adds the two judge dimensions, \emph{semantic}
(\ff{FF-N02}, intent alignment of a file with its layer's role) and \emph{integrity} (\ff{FF-N01}, module coherence:
no split concern, duplicated invariant or bypassed abstraction), with weight 0.04 each, so the judge dimensions weigh
$\Wn = 0.08$ in total.
% src: presets/nestjs.yaml (full_mode_weights: semantic 0.04, integrity 0.04); src/llm-critic/rubric.ts (FF-N01, FF-N02 rule, pass and fail text); Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.5 ("W_n = 0.08: semantic 0.04, integrity 0.04 in every registered spec")
The verdict is pass at $\geq 0.80$, warning at $\geq 0.65$, soft-block at $\geq 0.50$ and hard-block below.
% src: presets/nestjs.yaml (scoring.thresholds: pass 0.80, warning 0.65, soft_block 0.50)

\paragraph{The judge.}
For each judge function the gate draws up to 20 units per project, round-robin across layers, and asks the judge
three times per unit; a unit fails by majority. Under the registered rule a judge function fails only when strictly
more than half of its valid units fail, and only a failing function adds to its dimension's AVR.
% src: Docs/judge-preregistration.md (degradation ladder: "E7 unitCap 20"); Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.1 (majority-of-valid-units-v1); Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.2 ("the cap of 20 (BR-U4-SEL-04)")
The judge is a pinned Claude model at the top tier, called through the Claude Code CLI at a pinned version with no
tools, a frozen rubric whose hash is asserted by a test, and a dedicated configuration directory.
% src: Docs/judge-preregistration.md ("Instrument" table: judge model, effort high, PINNED_CLI_VERSION 2.1.294, --tools "", FROZEN_SHA256)
The rubric and parameters were frozen on 2026-10-09, before any measured judged run.
% src: Docs/judge-preregistration.md (Status: FROZEN 2026-10-09)

\paragraph{Data.}
\emph{E7}: nine real open-source TypeScript projects, 331 judged units (152 integrity, 179 intent), 993 judgements.
% src: results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_summary.csv (rows "*": FF-N01 152, FF-N02 179); Docs/ADR.md ADR-028 Context ("993 judged units across 9 real projects"; 993 = 331 x 3, derived)
\emph{E1}: projects generated by coding agents from two tasks at three specification levels, three runs each; 65
valid cells were judged (47 from three Claude tiers, 18 from one OpenAI model), 1\,233 judged units in total.
% src: results/e1-grid/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (65 rows, evaluation_mode full); results/e1-grid/agg/so5_grid.csv (generation_status ok: 18 + 18 + 11 Claude, 18 OpenAI; 7 failed-typecheck, all from the smallest Claude tier); results/e1-grid/judge-diagnostics/judge_summary.csv (rows "*": 300 + 933 = 1233 valid units, derived)
The E7 readings beyond the registered rule are post hoc; all E1 readings were registered before E1 ran.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

LLM-as-a-judge was popularised for chat evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging} and is known to show position bias
\citeneeded{wang2023fair}, self-preference \citeneeded{panickssery2024self,wataoka2024selfpref} and sensitivity to
prompt and model \citeneeded{thakur2024judging}. In software engineering, LLMs have been used to score code
\citeneeded{zhuo2024icescore} and to replace human annotators \citeneeded{ahmed2025annotation}. These studies
measure the judge in isolation. We study a judge embedded in a decision procedure, where weighting and aggregation
mediate its effect. For agreement we follow the kappa paradox literature \cite{feinstein1990high}\citeneeded{byrt1993bias}
and report Gwet's AC1 \cite{gwet2008ac1} beside Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960}. Isolation of LLM tools connects
to work on indirect prompt injection \citeneeded{greshake2023injection}.

\section{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}

\subsection{Lesson 1: the judge was active but inert}\label{sec:l1}

On E7 the judge failed 31 of 152 integrity units and 42 of 179 intent units, 73 of 331 (22\%). No unit was invalid
and no unit had a split vote.
% src: results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_summary.csv (rows "*": FF-N01 31 fail of 152 valid, FF-N02 42 of 179; units_invalid 0; units_split_vote 0); 73/331 = 0.221 derived
Yet no judge function reached ``fail'' on any project (the most was 9 failing units of 20), so both judge AVRs were
0 everywhere and the judge changed no verdict. On E1 it failed 55 of 1\,233 units and also changed none of the 65
verdicts, all of which were pass.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-028 Context ("the highest was 9 of 20 units"); results/e7-corpus/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (delta_judge_registered 0.000 and judge_mattered_registered false on 9 rows); results/e1-grid/judge-diagnostics/judge_summary.csv (rows "*": 36 + 19 = 55 fail, derived); results/e1-grid/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (judge_mattered_registered false on 65 rows; verdict_registered pass on 65 rows)

\paragraph{The bound.}
Write the combined score as its symbolic part plus the judge part. Let the \emph{judge-blind} score
$\ahs{blind}$ be $\ahs{comb}$ recomputed with every judge AVR set to 0. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:ahs},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bound}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{judge}} \;=\; \ahs{comb} - \ahs{blind} \;=\; -\sum_{n \in \text{judge}} w_n\, \mathrm{AVR}_n
  \;\in\; [-\Wn,\, 0] .
\end{equation}
The judge can lower the score by at most $\Wn$ and can never raise it. It can therefore move a verdict only when
$\ahs{blind}$ lies less than $\Wn$ above a threshold. This is a property of the configuration, decidable before any
judge call.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.3 (judge-effect decomposition: judge_bound = -W_n, ahs_combined_judge_blind, delta_judge in [-W_n, 0]); Docs/ADR.md ADR-028 "Review fixes (2026-10-10, prereg v15)"
On E7, only one of nine projects (dry-run, $\ahs{blind} = 0.547$, 0.047 above the soft-block threshold) was
reachable at $\Wn = 0.08$; five were already hard-blocked and the other three lay 0.09 or more above their
threshold. On E1, 4 of 65 cells had $\ahs{blind} < 0.88$ and were reachable; the largest drop the judge caused
under any aggregation was 0.068, and none crossed.
% src: results/e7-corpus/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (ahs_combined_judge_blind 0.547, 0.437, 0.411, 0.247, 0.743, 0.441, 0.591, 0.608, 0.443; reachability derived against thresholds 0.80/0.65/0.50 from presets/nestjs.yaml)
% src: results/e1-grid/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (ahs_combined_judge_blind min 0.852, 4 rows below 0.88, derived; ahs_combined_any_fail minus ahs_combined_judge_blind min -0.068, derived; delta_judge_registered min -0.029)

\paragraph{The decomposition.}
We registered a decomposition that is a pure function of the stored report:
$\ahs{comb} - \ahs{det} = \Delta_{\mathrm{dil}} + \Delta_{\mathrm{judge}}$, where
$\Delta_{\mathrm{dil}} = \ahs{blind} - \ahs{det}$ is a weighting effect (Lesson 3) and $\Delta_{\mathrm{judge}}$ is
the judge's own contribution. Table~\ref{tab:e7} shows it for E7: $\Delta_{\mathrm{judge}}$ is exactly 0 on all
nine projects, so the whole gap between the combined and the rules-only score is weighting.

\subsection{Lesson 2: aggregation and weight decide whether the judge matters}\label{sec:l2}

Because the registered rule needs a majority of failing units, a judge that fails a fifth of units almost never
produces a failing function. We registered two alternatives before E1: a \emph{proportional} rule, where a judge
function contributes the inclusion-weighted (H\'ajek \citeneeded{horvitz1952}) share of failed units, and a
\emph{judge-weighted} variant that rescales the weights so the two judge dimensions weigh 2/7, as two of seven
dimensions would; an \emph{any-fail} re-score (one failing unit fails the function) was reported beside them.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.2 (proportional-inclusion-weighted-v1, Hajek share); section 12.5 (judge-weighted-v1, 2/7 and 5/7); section 12.3 (verdict_any_fail, BR-U5b-60 any-fail re-score); Docs/ADR.md ADR-028 Decision and "Judge-weighted variant"
Table~\ref{tab:matter} counts the verdicts the judge moves, that is, where the verdict differs from the judge-blind
verdict under the same weights.

\subsection{Lesson 3: a clean judge dimension raises the score}\label{sec:l3}

Adding a dimension whose AVR is 0 cannot leave the score unchanged: it takes weight away from the symbolic
dimensions. If the full-mode symbolic weights are the rules-only weights scaled by $1-\Wn$, then
$\ahs{blind} = (1-\Wn)\,\ahs{det} + \Wn$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dil}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dil}} = \Wn\,(1 - \ahs{det}) \;\geq\; 0 .
\end{equation}
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.3 ("delta_dilution ... equals W_n * (1 - ahsDeterministic) ... when the full-mode symbolic weights are proportional to the symbolic-only ones, and approximately so otherwise")
The worse the project, the larger the bonus. On E7 the combined score was 0.022 to 0.072 above the rules-only score
for this reason alone (Table~\ref{tab:e7}); the identity predicts 0.022 to 0.066, the residue coming from
renormalisation over executed dimensions. On E1 the effect was at most 0.015, because the symbolic AVRs were low.
% src: results/e7-corpus/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (delta_dilution 0.022 to 0.072; dilution_identity 0.022 to 0.066); results/e1-grid/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (delta_dilution max 0.015)
The effect grows with the weight. At 2/7, reweighting alone, with the judge contributing nothing, moves 7 of 9 E7
verdicts upward (for example realworld from warning to pass); the judge-blind score is 0.079 to 0.240 above
$\ahs{det}$.
% src: results/e7-corpus/agg-proportional/ahs_by_aggregation.csv (verdict_changed_jw_registered true on 7 rows; realworld-test verdict_registered warning, verdict_jw_registered pass; ahs_combined_jw_judge_blind minus ahs_deterministic, 0.079 to 0.240, derived)
A reader who sees $\ahs{comb} > \ahs{det}$ might conclude that the judge found the code healthier than the rules
did. It found nothing of the kind: the gap is arithmetic.

\subsection{Lesson 4: coding judge rationales can mislead}\label{sec:l4}

To learn \emph{what} the judge finds, we registered a mechanical coding frame: each fail clause of the rubric is a
list of keyword patterns, and a rationale is coded with every clause it matches.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.4 (coding frame, machine block judge-coding-frame)
Frame 1.0.0 coded the judge's free-text reasoning. It did not separate failing from passing units, because the judge
walks through every criterion and negates most of them (``no duplicated rules or invariants, and does not bypass any
abstraction'' in a passing unit). The duplication code matched 119 of 121 passing integrity units but 30 of 31
failing ones; the persistence code matched 133 of 137 passing intent units against 35 of 42 failing ones.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.4 ("Frame 1.0.0 ... did not separate fail from pass rationales (the judge's reasoning walks every rubric criterion and negates most of them)"); results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_units.csv (rationale of dry-run-test, src/session/infrastructure/persistence/relational, verdict pass, quoted); git show 9eda300a:results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_criteria.csv (rows "*": FF-N01 pass JC-N01-DUP 119/121, fail 30/31; FF-N02 pass JC-N02-PERSISTENCE 133/137, fail 35/42)
Frame 1.1.0 codes only the violation messages, which the judge writes only when it fails a unit: they were present
on 73 of 73 failing carriers and 0 of 258 passing ones. A guarded coding of the reasoning (drop a match preceded by
a negation within five tokens) is kept as secondary.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.4 ("Frame 1.1.0 ... present on 73 of 73 failing carriers and on 0 of 258 passing carriers"; negationGuard window 5)
The guard is only partial: 78 of 121 passing integrity units still match the duplication code. With the
pre-declared discrimination statistic (failing-message share minus guarded passing share, informative at
$\geq 0.3$), one code was informative on E7 (duplication, 0.323, post hoc), and none on E1 (highest 0.258), where the
reading was pre-registered.
% src: results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_criteria.csv (FF-N01, *, pass, JC-N01-DUP, units_coded_reasoning_guarded 78 of 121); results/e7-corpus/judge-diagnostics/judge_discrimination.csv (rows "*": JC-N01-DUP 0.323114 informative true, all others false); results/e1-grid/judge-diagnostics/judge_discrimination.csv (rows "*": all informative false, max JC-N02-TRANSPORT 0.258378)
A frame written from the rubric measures the rubric's vocabulary, which a careful judge repeats whatever its
verdict. Code what the judge asserts as a violation, not what it discusses.

\subsection{Lesson 5: the judge mostly re-finds what the rules find}\label{sec:l5}

For every failing unit we checked whether any of its files also carries a symbolic violation in the same report (a
mechanical join on file path). On E7, 63 of 73 judge-failed units were also flagged by the rules (25 of 31 integrity,
38 of 42 intent); on E1 all 55 were.

\subsection{Lesson 6: self-preference signals are confounded with code quality}\label{sec:l6}

The judge is the same model as the top-tier Claude generator in E1, so self-preference is a known risk
\citeneeded{panickssery2024self}. We pre-registered a directional check: per cell
$d = \ahs{neur} - \ahs{det}$, comparing the judge's own model with the other Claude tiers.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md section 6 ("Directional self-preference check (registered, ADR-020 item 7)"); Docs/ADR.md ADR-020 item 7
It came out positive: mean difference $+0.083$, one-sided $p = 0.014$, Cliff's $\delta = 0.20$.
% src: results/e1-grid/agg/so5_tests.csv (directional:claude-opus-5-5-vs-others: statistic 0.083048, p_raw 0.013699, cliffs_delta 0.197318)
By the registered wording this is evidence of self-preference. The cells tell a different story. Mean $d$ is
$+0.083$ for the top tier, $+0.088$ for the middle tier and $-0.144$ for the smallest tier; the two larger tiers
have $\ahs{neur} = 1.000$ in every cell, while the smallest averages 0.777. Their rules-only scores are alike
(0.917, 0.912, 0.921), and the model effect on $\ahs{det}$ is null ($p = 0.753$) while that on $\ahs{neur}$ is
large (Holm $p = 0.0003$).
% src: results/e1-grid/agg/so5_grid.csv (per requested_model_id: mean of ahs_neuronal - ahs_deterministic, mean ahs_neuronal, mean ahs_deterministic; derived); results/e1-grid/agg/so5_tests.csv (model, co-primary:ahsDeterministic p_holm 0.753225; model, secondary:ahsNeuronal p_holm 0.000300)
The judge failed 33 of 209 units from the smallest tier, 2 of 450 from the top tier and 2 of 344 from the middle

% src: results/e1-grid/judge-diagnostics/judge_units.csv (verdict fail and unit counts by generator prefix of project_id; derived)

smallest tier is also an older generation and its 11 valid cells are survivors of a type-check that 7 of its 18

which with one OpenAI model is not evidence of absence.
% src: results/e1-grid/agg/so5_grid.csv (claude-haiku-4-5: 11 ok, 7 failed-typecheck); Docs/threats-to-validity.md TV-49 ("Tier is confounded with generation"); results/e1-grid/agg/so5_tests.csv (directional:anthropic-vs-other-vendor p_raw 0.172683); Docs/analysis-plan.md section 6.1 ("A null result is not evidence of no self-preference")

field alone. Each unit was judged three times, as before: 300 calls, none invalid.

\subsection{Lesson 9: construct validity of synthetic judge probes}\label{sec:l9}

To measure the judge's detection on known violations we wrote two seed operators, one per judge function, each with
a twin that should stay clean. They were frozen before use. On real code they found nothing to seed: on the seven
held-out corpus projects every attempt ended with no site, on six of them with no candidate site at all.
% src: results/so4-heldout/agg/seed_coverage.csv (MO-X02, MO-X03: 7 rows each, stage mutate, status rejected, reason no-site; candidates 0 on six projects; nestjslatam__ddd candidates refused by not-removable-guard)
A later count over the 65 accepted generated (E1) cells, made without reading any detector output or calling any
model, found the same: the two seed operators place no site in any cell. One operator had no candidate site in 59
cells and the other in 51; where candidates existed, every one was refused by a precondition (the guarded method
could not be removed, or its type shape did not fit). Only twins could be placed: 12 twin rows in 11 cells.
% src: Docs/DiagnosticRuns/judge-probe-e1-site-count.md (MO-X02 rows 0, 59 cells with 0 candidates, 3 cells all rejected; MO-X03 rows 0, 51 cells with 0 candidates, 11 cells all rejected; twins MO-X02n 1 row and MO-X03n 11 rows, 12 rows in 11 cells)
The reason is the construction. Both seeds need a boolean entity method that is used only as the condition of
guard statements (\texttt{if (!e.m()) throw}). Neither the real projects nor the agents' code look like that: they
validate inline, or their methods do more than return a boolean, or the type checker will not let the method be
removed. The probes therefore encode the authors' picture of a semantic violation, not a construct that occurs in
the code the judge is asked to read. Under the frozen operators the judge's detection rate cannot be measured on
either population; only the twins' false-alarm rate can. The follow-up detection experiment is on hold until new
operators exist.
% src: Docs/DiagnosticRuns/judge-probe-e1-site-count.md ("Reading": boolean entity method used only as guard condition; generated code validates inline; "Under the frozen operators the judge's detection rate cannot be measured on either population. Only the twin false-alarm rate can."); Docs/ADR.md ADR-038 Status (ADR-037 reserved, on hold for new operators)
Before freezing a synthetic probe, count its sites on the target population, real and generated, and treat a probe
that cannot be placed as a failed instrument rather than a passed judge.

\subsection{Lesson 10: operating CLI judges and labellers}\label{sec:l8}

\paragraph{The judge's CLI writes more than you expect.}
Using a subscription CLI rather than an API brought hidden state. A probe of the judge configuration directory
found that login had installed a plugin marketplace (about 786 entries), that the CLI creates a
\texttt{settings.json} and a per-project memory directory on every call even with session persistence off, and
that the CLI had auto-updated from 2.1.293 to 2.1.294 during login. An authentication error reported the subtype
``success''.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-018 Context (plugins/** about 786 entries; settings.json; projects/<cwd>/memory even with --no-session-persistence; auto-update 2.1.293 to 2.1.294) and Decision item 5 ("an auth error reports subtype: success")
We removed the plugins, allowed only empty memory directories and a two-key settings file, disabled the updater,
pinned the version and made every run fail closed on a mismatch. One channel (a user-level instruction file) could
not be positively controlled and remains a residual threat.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-018 Decision items 1 to 4 and 6; Docs/judge-preregistration.md ("Instrument" table, Isolation and Claude CLI version rows)

\paragraph{A deny rule that does not deny.}
The panel first ran through an agentic Gemini CLI, with tools made inert by a strict permission mode and seven deny
rules. Its first validation call timed out twice and its re-ask then completed a web search; a fail-closed guard
stopped the run before the answer was stored. Probes showed that web search passes no permission check on that CLI
version: neither a list of 51 disabled tools nor explicit deny rules stopped it.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-034 Context (two 240 s timeouts, search_web on the re-ask, LLM_CLI_ISOLATION stop; disabledTools of 51 tools, init listed 60; deny rules added, search_web completed again); Docs/labeller-route.md section 7
Earlier labelling on the same route (142 invocations) had used no tool, so the guard had never been exercised.
% src: Docs/labeller-route.md section 7 ("P1/MS/P2: verified ... all 142 invocations ... No call there completed a tool step")

\paragraph{Fail closed, and decide in advance when to stop.}
Rather than patching around the leak, we registered a rule before continuing: a call that completes a tool step is
discarded and retried once; if 20\% or more of the first ten or more items end as tool use, the run stops and the
route goes back to the author. It fired: 2 of the first 10 items ended as tool use, after 5 discarded attempts. The
author moved the panel to the vendor API with no tools and temperature 0, relabelled all 46 items with zero tool use
and zero retries, and kept the 8 earlier answers apart.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-034 Decision items 1 to 3; Docs/ADR.md ADR-035 Context ("2 of the first 10 P4 items ended as uncertain / tool-use, with 5 tool-use discards") and Decision items 1, 2, 4; results/labels/live-call-ledger.md (row 5: 0 retries, 0 re-asks, 0 tool use, 0 quota stops)
The price is a route difference between the panel's earlier labels and the validation labels, which we report as a
threat.
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-035 Consequences; Docs/labeller-route.md section 8
Agentic CLIs are built to act, and a judge or labeller must not; the safest configuration is an API with no tools
offered, and where a CLI is unavoidable, a guard that inspects what the model actually did.

\section{v1.3: Measuring What the Judge Catches}\label{sec:v13}

% Provenance for this section: every number carries a "% src:" comment naming a committed file on branch v1.3.

Lessons 1 to 10 come from Study 1, the registered v1.2E evaluation. Study 1 left the central question open: its
robustness check showed that the judge's unit verdicts barely depend on the model (agreement 0.94, $\kappa = 0.77$,
no verdict change; Lesson 8), and its panel check showed that the judge does not fail sound code (AC1 0.98 on 36
generated units; Lesson 7); but the panel failed only 2 of those units and the probes meant to test fail detection
placed no seed (Lesson 9). So Study 1 never measured whether the judge \emph{catches} a violation.

On the less circular hand-written analogues all three instruments agree. On duplicated rules in generated code the
order reverses: the judge fails all nine seeds, the proxies flag two, the symbolic rules none. The clone proxy misses
because its filters, added to silence false flags on clean code, drop bare guards and ignore clones inside one

\emph{Lesson:} where an anti-pattern is a type or call fact, a deterministic proxy finds it faster and more reliably
than the judge; where it is a judgement about responsibility or duplication, the judge sees what the proxies'
filters remove.

\subsection{A layered neuro-symbolic gate}\label{sec:layered}

These results suggest a division of labour, which v1.3 adopts as a design and has not yet evaluated end to end.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Symbolic rules decide.} Only rules that state a defining constraint of the style as a structural
        fact can block; on the v1.3 development set an independent checker found none of their checkable findings
        incorrect. % src: Docs/v1.3/rule-tiers.md (Principle); Docs/DiagnosticRuns/v1.3-labelling-2026-10-15.md §2 (54 of 54 blocking checkable findings correct)
  \item \textbf{Cheap proxies propose.} Deterministic detectors emit candidate sites with evidence, in
        milliseconds, and give no verdict. % src: Docs/v1.3/cheap-semantics-design.md ("A proxy proposes candidate sites. It does not give a verdict. The later LLM pass is meant to verify the candidates.")
  \item \textbf{The judge interprets.} It reads the candidates and the units no catalogue covers, and in v1.3 it is
        reported but does not block. % src: Docs/v1.3/rule-tiers.md ("In v1.3 the LLM judge dimensions (FF-N01, FF-N02) are reported and do not block")
\end{enumerate}
This keeps the judge out of the verdict until its validity is shown, which is Lesson 1's warning turned into a
design rule. Whether the judge verifies a proxy's candidate better than it judges blind has not been measured.
% src: Docs/DiagnosticRuns/cheap-semantics-2026-10-15.md §7 ("Not measured ... Whether the LLM pass, given the proxy's candidate and evidence, verifies better than it judges blind")

\subsection{Threat: same-vendor correlation}\label{sec:samevendor}

The frozen judge and the alternative judge of Lesson 8 are two models of one vendor, and in round 2 fourteen of the
sixteen operator seeds sit in code generated by models of that same vendor. Shared training can make the judge and
the generator wrong in the same way, and the two judges wrong together: their agreement is model stability, not
validity, and a blind spot shared with the generator would lower the catch rate on its code without showing up in
any agreement figure. The only cross-vendor check, the Study 1 panel, barely tested fail detection, and the planned
cross-vendor meaningfulness labeller stopped at its usage limit before producing labels. Human labels are the
missing control. % src: Docs/reports/director-report-2026-10.md §4.10 Limits ("shared training can make both wrong in the same way (TV-114)"); Docs/DiagnosticRuns/v1.3-judge-catch-round2-2026-10-15.md §1a Spread (sonnet 8, opus 4, haiku 2, gpt-5.6-terra 2; 14 of 16 derived); Docs/DiagnosticRuns/v1.3-labelling-2026-10-15.md §3 (Codex labeller usage limit)

\section{Recommendations for LLM-Judge Gates}\label{sec:recs}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Compute the reach before the run.} State $\Wn$, the bound $[-\Wn, 0]$ and which items are reachable
        (Eq.~\ref{eq:bound}). If the intended role of the judge is to move verdicts, check that the configuration
        allows it (Lessons 1, 2).
  \item \textbf{Register the aggregation, and a sensitivity set.} Majority, proportional and any-fail rules give
        different answers from the same calls; report them side by side from recorded calls (Lesson 2).
  \item \textbf{Report the judge-blind score and the decomposition}, never only $\ahs{comb}$ against $\ahs{det}$
        (Lessons 1, 3).
  \item \textbf{Code what the judge asserts}, its violation messages, not its reasoning; measure discrimination
        against passing units (Lesson 4).
  \item \textbf{Join judge failures with rule findings} and report the judge-only residue (Lesson 5).
  \item \textbf{Check self-preference against an external label}, and against a non-self tier of the same family
        (Lesson 6).
  \item \textbf{Validate on fails.} Stratify the validation sample by judge verdict; report AC1 beside $\kappa$ and
        agreement on fails separately (Lesson 7).
  \item \textbf{Treat isolation as testable.} Pin versions, fail closed on drift, prefer APIs without tools, and
        pre-register stop rules for when isolation fails (Lesson 10).
  \item \textbf{Replay with another model.} Recorded prompts make a model swap cheap; report agreement, its
        direction and the verdict impact (Lesson 8).
  \item \textbf{Count probe sites before freezing a probe}, on real and on generated code (Lesson 9).
  \item \textbf{Build twins that change only the construct}, and read the judge's reasons before crediting a catch
        (Lesson 11).
  \item \textbf{Let cheap deterministic proxies propose and the judge interpret}, and keep the judge out of the
        blocking verdict until its catches are validated by humans (Lesson 12, Section~\ref{sec:layered}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\emph{Construct.} The panel is one model family; no human audited the validation items, and LLM-panel agreement is
not ground truth \citeneeded{ahmed2025annotation}. The coding frames are keyword maps written by the author.
% src: results/e1-grid/label-plan-p4-api/labelling.json (panel-vs-audit n 0); Docs/analysis-plan.md section 12.4 ("Limits")
\emph{Internal.} All E7 readings beyond the registered rule, frame 1.1.0 on E7 and the judge-weighted variant on E7
were defined after E7 results were known; we mark them post hoc. Generator tier is confounded with model
generation, and the panel route changed between label sets.
% src: Docs/analysis-plan.md sections 12 and 12.5 (post-hoc E7 readings); Docs/threats-to-validity.md TV-49; Docs/ADR.md ADR-035 Consequences
\emph{External.} One gate, one judge model (checked against one alternative model of the same vendor on 100
units), two rubrics, nine real projects and two generation tasks; the bound argument generalises, the counts do
not. The judge has no seeded ground truth, because its probes could not be placed (Lesson 9).
% src: Docs/ADR.md ADR-038 Consequences (TV-114); Docs/DiagnosticRuns/judge-probe-e1-site-count.md
\emph{Development data (v1.3).} The catch test and the three-way comparison use planted, textbook anti-patterns on
development data with no pre-registration; ten of the eleven round-1 seeds sit on one small fixture, the cheap
proxies were written after the round-1 results were read, and the duplicated-rule catch is confounded
(Lesson 11). The judge and its generators share a vendor (Section~\ref{sec:samevendor}).

\emph{Conclusion.} Validation samples are small ($n = 36$ on the headline) and its intervals are wide; the
self-preference check is one test.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

An LLM judge inside a gate can be active, plausible and validated by a panel, and still decide nothing. In our gate
the judge failed about a fifth of real-project units, explained them well, mostly agreed with an independent panel
and with a second model of its own vendor, yet the registered weight and aggregation left it no verdict to move, and a clean judge dimension made scores
look better. Whether the judge ``matters'' was settled by configuration choices that can be analysed before any call
is made. We recommend that gate designers make those choices explicit, register them with a sensitivity set, report
the judge-blind decomposition, and validate the judge where it is supposed to act: on the units it fails. Our first attempt at that, on planted
anti-patterns, shows a judge that catches most of them and is fooled by few twins, yet whose best catch cannot be
attributed to the construct it names; until human labels exist, the judge should interpret and the rules should
decide.
